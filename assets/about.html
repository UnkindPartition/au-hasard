What does Au hasard mean?

It’s French for “at random”, pronounced “oh ah-ZAR” (/o a.zaʁ/).

What does mean?

It’s the mathematical notation for the uniform distribution between a and b: every value from a to b is equally likely. That’s exactly what Au hasard does.

Are the results truly random?

Yes. Au hasard uses two sources of randomness:

  • Your phone’s operating system. The Linux kernel collects unpredictable physical events, such as the precise timing of hardware interrupts and, on most modern phones, the output of a hardware random number generator built into the chip. The app takes its starting point from the kernel’s random number generator.
  • You. Each time you press Draw or Shuffle, the precise moment of your tap, down to the nanosecond, is mixed in.

The results are unpredictable as long as either source is. Even if the phone’s generator were flawed, or deliberately weakened, the timing of your taps would keep the draws from being predicted or reproduced by anyone outside your phone.

Apart from being unpredictable, fair draws also need to be statistically correct: every outcome equally likely, and each draw independent of the ones before it. Au hasard’s methods, described below in more detail, are thoroughly verified with statistical tests: chi-square tests of uniformity and of independence between consecutive draws, and a runs test for trends.

How does it work, exactly?

When the app starts, a 256-bit key is read from the kernel’s random number generator, /dev/urandom. Before each draw or shuffle, the key is replaced with the SHA-256 hash of the previous key and the time of your tap in nanoseconds. From the current key, the cipher ChaCha20 produces the stream of random 64-bit numbers from which the draws and shuffles below are made.

Drawing numbers. To draw from a range of n = b − a + 1 numbers, the app takes a random 64-bit number x and returns a + (x mod  n). Done naively, this would be slightly biased: the 264 possible values of x can’t always be split evenly among n results, so some results would come up a tiny bit more often. The app avoids this by discarding the 264 mod  n smallest values of x and drawing again when it gets one of them (rejection sampling). The remaining values split evenly.

Shuffling lists. Lists are shuffled with the Fisher–Yates algorithm, which fills the positions one by one, each with an item chosen at random from those not yet placed.

Shuffling numbers. Shuffle on the Numbers tab shows up to 100 different numbers from the range, in random order. Writing out a huge range just to shuffle it would be impossible, so the numbers are chosen with Robert Floyd’s sampling algorithm, which needs only as many steps as numbers chosen, and then put in random order with Fisher–Yates.

What about privacy?

Au hasard works fully offline. The app doesn’t even have permission to use the Internet, so nothing you enter or draw can leave your phone. Likewise, it doesn’t collect or transmit any analytics or other data.
