What does “au hasard” mean?

It’s French for “at random”.

What does mean?

It’s the mathematical notation for the uniform distribution between a and b: every value from a to b is equally likely. That’s exactly what Au hasard does.

Are the results truly random?

Au hasard gets its randomness from your phone’s operating system: the cryptographically secure random number generator of the Linux kernel, the same source that is used to generate encryption keys. The kernel collects unpredictable physical events, such as the precise timing of hardware interrupts and, on most modern phones, the output of a hardware random number generator built into the chip. From these it seeds a generator whose output can’t be predicted or reproduced by anyone, including the author of this app.

But random.org says these numbers are pseudo-random and «generated in a predictable fashion»!

That’s true of ordinary pseudo-random number generators, like the ones built into most programming languages: they’re simple formulas, so after seeing a few outputs you can work out all the following ones.

Au hasard’s numbers are pseudo-random too, but in a much stronger sense. They come from a cryptographically secure generator, which is designed so that its output can’t be predicted, even by someone who has seen any number of previous outputs, without knowing its internal state. That state is seeded from physical events, like random.org’s numbers, is constantly refreshed with more of them, and never leaves your phone’s operating system. Predicting it would mean breaking the cryptography that protects encryption keys, including the ones that keep your connection to random.org private.

In practice, for drawing a number, a winner or an order, there’s no difference you could detect: both sources are unpredictable and unbiased. One thing random.org does offer is draws recorded by a third party, so that others can check that a draw was fair. If you need that, for example for an official lottery, use such a service.

What about privacy?

Au hasard is built to respect it:

  • It works fully offline. The app doesn’t even have permission to use the Internet, so nothing you enter or draw can leave your phone.
  • No ads, no tracking, no analytics, no account.
  • Your saved lists are stored only on your phone, and excluded from cloud backups.
  • It’s free software: anyone can read the source code and check all of the above.

How does it work?

The source of randomness is Java’s SecureRandom, which on Android is provided by BoringSSL and seeded from the Linux kernel’s random number generator.

Drawing numbers. To draw from a range of n = b − a + 1 numbers, the app takes a random 64-bit number x and returns a + (x mod n). Done naively, this would be slightly biased: the 264 possible values of x can’t always be split evenly among n results, so some results would come up a tiny bit more often. The app avoids this by discarding the 264 mod n smallest values of x and drawing again when it gets one of them. The remaining values split evenly.

Shuffling and picking. Lists are shuffled with the Fisher–Yates algorithm, which fills the positions one by one, each with an item chosen at random from those not yet placed. Picking items stops after the first positions. Distinct numbers from a range are chosen with Robert Floyd’s sampling algorithm, which is fast even for huge ranges, and then shuffled.

Testing. The algorithms are checked with statistical tests: chi-square tests of uniformity and of independence between consecutive draws, and a runs test. Each test is repeated with 2,000 different seeds, and the spread of its outcomes is tested in turn.
